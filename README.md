Economist (PhD, Georgetown, 2024).

This repo is a public notebook of my personal research in progress — independent work, not affiliated with any employer. **Write-ups only, no code.** The NashBench evaluation code itself lives in a private repo; what is here is the design, the current state of the work, and where it goes next.

## Research interests

The throughline of my work is understanding how the economy changes as technologies develop — and what policy should do about it.

My dissertation studied the last wave: the proliferation of data sharing and of powerful AI algorithms in recommendation systems, and what they did to competition and welfare in digital markets. I built a framework to measure algorithmic search power, tested it on JD.com, and showed that the welfare cost of data sharing runs through market power, not personalized pricing.

I am now interested in the next wave: the economic-system implications of large language models — how they change markets, firms, and work, and what that means for competition, openness, and safety. NashBench is the empirical side of that question: whether the models now making strategic decisions can actually find the optima that human managers routinely reach.

All of this research is for applied policy. The aim is measurement and evidence that a regulator, a legislator, or a lab can use — not theory for its own sake.
